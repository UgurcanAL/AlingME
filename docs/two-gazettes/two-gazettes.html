Directive 2010/63/EU × 5199 & HADYEK

Two Gazettes

Comparing the EU's Official Journal and Türkiye's Resmî Gazete on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes, article by madde.

Format Comparative regulatory analysis Citation APA 7th Audience Regulatory affairs & research compliance
Abstract

This paper compares the European Union's Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes against Türkiye's implementing framework: the Deneysel ve Diğer Bilimsel Amaçlar İçin Kullanılan Hayvanların Refah ve Korunmasına Dair Yönetmelik, the HADYEK (Hayvan Deneyleri Yerel Etik Kurulu) ethics-committee structure, and 5199 sayılı Hayvanları Koruma Kanunu. Article-level analysis shows that Turkish law transposes the Directive's substantive standards — scope, definitions, the 3Rs principle, severity classification, and personnel-competence categories — with unusual fidelity. The comparison instead finds daylight at the institutional layer: HADYEK's governing regulation (2014) can now be identified, but no published source reports the project-evaluation criteria or decision timeline it applies; a second, parallel committee system governs aquatic vertebrates and cephalopods; the severe-pain exception sits with individual local ethics committees rather than requiring state-level, Commission-notified authorization; and enforcement carries no verified inspection-frequency floor and a per-animal fine schedule that escalates for repeat offences and for veterinary or animal-care staff but never with a facility's size or resources. A full-text reading of Law No. 5199 also shows that the Kanun carries more of the framework than is usually credited to it: its own definition of “deney,” a statutory care-and-protection duty alongside the replacement principle, the enabling basis for establishment registration, personnel qualifications, record-keeping and notification duties, and a veterinarian-only rule for surgical interventions whose reach into research practice is unresolved. The paper argues that, as a result, compliance in Turkish facilities is driven less by domestic legal obligation than by sponsor and accreditation pressure — a distinction with direct implications for regulatory-affairs due diligence.

01

Why the Comparison Matters

Türkiye's framework for laboratory animal use is structurally similar to Directive 2010/63/EU, but it diverges precisely where the EU regime binds: severity classification, project-level authorisation, and personnel competence. The practical consequence is that compliance in Turkish facilities depends more on sponsor and accreditation pressure than on domestic legal obligation. This divergence is not merely academic. It determines whether data generated in a Turkish facility will be accepted without qualification by a European or American regulator, whether a multinational sponsor can rely on local ethics approval as a substitute for its own audit, and whether a Turkish researcher's training is portable across borders.

Directive 2010/63/EU falls within accession Chapter 12 (Food Safety, Veterinary and Phytosanitary Policy), screened in 2006–2007 and opened for negotiation in 2010 (European Commission, 2007). Accession negotiations have been effectively stalled since 2018, with no chapter opened or closed since; and yet legal and operational alignment with the Directive persists regardless, sustained by the commercial and institutional channels described below rather than by accession leverage.

The more immediate driver is commercial and institutional. Multinational sponsors conducting or outsourcing preclinical work expect data defensible under the standards their own regulators apply, regardless of where the work was performed; a Turkish facility working with such sponsors typically absorbs EU- or GLP-equivalent expectations contractually, not because domestic law requires it. International benchmarks reinforce this asymmetry unevenly. AAALAC International accreditation exists in Türkiye but appears limited: its own accreditation directory lists a single Turkish institution, the Acıbadem Mehmet Ali Aydınlar University Laboratory Animal Application and Research Center (AAALAC International, n.d., directory accessed August 26, 2026). FELASA's own education and training guidance shows no systemic pathway linking HADYEK-issued competence certification to FELASA function categories; delivery in Türkiye has instead been ad hoc, exemplified by a 2013 joint course between Utrecht University and Dokuz Eylül University (FELASA, n.d., accessed August 26, 2026). The result is a compliance landscape shaped less by statute than by which sponsors, accreditors, and export markets a given facility answers to. This is the argument the paper develops through Sections 3 to 7.

The closest earlier comparison, Gölcü and Aksoy's overview of EU and Turkish laboratory-animal legislation, reached a compatible conclusion from the text alone: parts of both the welfare yönetmelik and the ethics-committee yönetmelik were “translated almost word for word” from the Directive, and without adaptation to Türkiye's own conditions and wider legal framework they may not give animals an adequate basis of protection (Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017). That overview described the instruments section by section. This paper adds an article-by-madde comparison, a full reading of Law No. 5199, and a focus on the institutional layer where the two regimes actually part.

02

Scope and Definitions

EU The EU Position

Directive 2010/63/EU applies “where animals are used or intended to be used in procedures, or bred specifically so that their organs or tissues may be used for scientific purposes” (Directive 2010/63/EU, art 1(2)). The animals covered are defined narrowly but not exclusively by taxonomy: live non-human vertebrates, including independently feeding larval forms and foetal forms of mammals from the last third of their normal development, plus live cephalopods (art 1(3)). The inclusion of cephalopods, a class of roughly 700 invertebrate species, was a deliberate extension beyond the Directive's predecessor and the first time an entire invertebrate class entered laboratory-animal legislation across the EU (Fiorito et al., 2015); no comparable extension to any other invertebrate taxon was made.

The Directive does not define its scope by species alone but by the procedure performed. A “procedure” is “any use, invasive or non-invasive, of an animal for experimental or other scientific purposes, with known or unknown outcome, or educational purposes, which may cause the animal a level of pain, suffering, distress or lasting harm equivalent to, or higher than, that caused by the introduction of a needle in accordance with good veterinary practice” (art 3(1)). This is a comparator standard, not a fixed severity floor: it draws the line between what is regulated at all and what falls outside the Directive entirely, prior to any severity classification under Article 15 (addressed in Section 4).

Article 1(5) excludes six categories from scope regardless of species: non-experimental agricultural practices; non-experimental clinical veterinary practices; veterinary clinical trials conducted for the marketing authorisation of a veterinary medicinal product; practices undertaken for recognised animal husbandry purposes; practices whose primary purpose is animal identification; and any practice that does not meet the needle-prick threshold above (art 1(5)(a)–(f)).

The killing-for-tissue case is handled by a specific carve-out rather than silence. Breeding an animal specifically so its organs or tissues can be used for scientific purposes brings that animal within the Directive's scope under Article 1(2), meaning breeding establishments, care standards, and record-keeping obligations apply. But the act of killing itself is expressly excluded from the definition of “procedure” (art 3(1), final clause). Killing is nonetheless not unregulated: Article 6 requires that animals be killed with minimum pain, suffering and distress, in an authorised establishment, by a competent person, using a method appropriate to the species as set out in Annex IV (art 6(1)–(3)). The result is a two-track structure: killing-for-tissue escapes project authorisation and severity classification as a “procedure,” but does not escape the Directive's welfare and competence requirements entirely.

TR The Turkish Position

The yönetmelik's scope and definitions provisions track Directive 2010/63/EU closely enough that they read as a direct transposition rather than an independent drafting effort. Madde 2(1)(c) brings within scope “insan-dışı canlı omurgalılar” (live non-human vertebrates), independently feeding larval forms, and mammalian foetuses that have passed the first two-thirds of gestation (i.e., are in the final third of normal development), together with live cephalopods, matching Directive art 1(3) point for point, cephalopod inclusion and developmental threshold both intact. Madde 2(1)(ç) extends scope to animals at an earlier developmental stage where the animal will be allowed to survive beyond that stage and is liable to suffer as a result after reaching it, the same construction as Directive art 1(4). Madde 2(1)(b) provides that the yönetmelik applies until an animal is killed, rehomed, released to its natural habitat, or returned to a livestock system, and that successful elimination of pain via anaesthesia, analgesia, or other methods does not remove the use from scope, again matching Directive art 1(2).

The definitional layer shows the same pattern. Madde 4(s) defines “prosedür” using language nearly identical to Directive art 3(1): an invasive or non-invasive use of an animal for experimental, other scientific, or educational purposes, with known or unknown outcome, capable of causing pain, suffering, distress, or lasting harm equivalent to or greater than that caused by inserting a needle under good veterinary practice. As in the EU text, it expressly excludes “hayvanların yalnızca organları veya dokularını kullanmak için öldürülmesi” (killing animals solely to use their organs or tissues). Madde 4(r) defines “proje” as a work programme containing one or more procedures with a specific scientific purpose, mirroring Directive art 3(2). The exclusions in madde 2(2), non-experimental agricultural and clinical veterinary practice, field trials for veterinary product licensing, obligations of registered livestock enterprises, animal-identification practices, and the needle-prick threshold itself, map onto all six categories in Directive art 1(5)(a)–(f).

Sitting above the yönetmelik, Law No. 5199 carries its own, older definitional layer, and it does not map onto the Directive as neatly. Kanun art 3(l) defines “deney” as the use of any animal (“herhangi bir hayvanın”) for experimental or other scientific purposes that will cause pain, suffering, distress or lasting harm (“acı, eziyet, üzüntü veya uzun süreli hasara neden olacak”), and art 3(m) defines “deney hayvanı” as any animal used or to be used in an experiment. Three differences from Directive art 3(1) follow from the text: the statutory definition has no taxonomic limit (the Kanun elsewhere defines wild animals to include invertebrates, art 3(h)), it has no needle-prick comparator, and it does not mention educational purposes or “known or unknown outcome.” The Directive-aligned scope (vertebrates, cephalopods, the developmental thresholds, the needle-prick line) exists only at yönetmelik level. The practical point is narrow but real: the Kanun's own requirements in art 9, scientific purpose only, use only where no other option exists, and ethics-committee authorisation, are written for “hayvanlar” without a species boundary, so their outer limit is set by the implementing regulation rather than by the statute.

The killing-for-tissue case is also handled partly in the Kanun rather than only in the yönetmelik. Art 8(2) prohibits removing or destroying all or part of a living animal's organs or tissues other than for medical purposes; art 14(1)(e) prohibits any non-therapeutic intervention on an animal's body before its death has been definitively confirmed; and art 13(4) makes the persons and bodies responsible for killing liable for confirming death and for proper disposal of the carcass, with killing and euthanasia methods delegated to a Ministry yönetmelik. Art 13(3) bars killing animals during birth, pregnancy and lactation, but expressly excepts “tıbbî ve bilimsel gerekçeler” (medical and scientific grounds). The art 8(2), art 14(1)(e) and art 13(4) rules each carry their own per-animal fine (art 28(d), (j), (ı) respectively), and for art 13(4) the fine rises from 2,200 to 5,500 TRY per animal where the breach is committed by an establishment (“işletmelerce”). The Turkish system therefore reproduces the EU's two-track structure, with killing outside the “procedure” definition but still regulated, and anchors part of the second track in primary legislation.

The practical implication is that scope and definitions are not where this comparison finds daylight. A hiring manager evaluating a Turkish facility's compliance posture should not expect ambiguity here: what counts as a regulated animal, a regulated procedure, or an excluded practice is essentially the same question with the same answer under both regimes. The divergence the thesis identifies, at authorisation, severity classification, and personnel competence, begins in Section 3.

One structural feature of the Turkish scope has no EU counterpart. Since 2019, aquatic vertebrates have been carved out into a separate instrument, the Bilimsel Amaçlar İçin Kullanılan Sucul Omurgalı Canlıların Refah ve Korunmasına Dair Yönetmelik (Resmî Gazete, 20 April 2019; amended 23 December 2022 and 27 April 2024). It covers fish, amphibians and aquatic reptiles (cetaceans excepted), cyclostomes and cephalopods, and runs its own establishment permits and its own central and local ethics committees under the Ministry's Directorate-General of Fisheries and Aquaculture (Demirel & Uluköy, 2025, pp. 116–117). The Directive-matching scope described above is therefore split across two Turkish regulations, not one, and the Turkish home of cephalopod protection is the aquatic instrument. This matters in practice because fish were the largest single group of animals used in Türkiye in 2020, at 37% (Demirel & Uluköy, 2025, p. 121).

Authorisation and Project Evaluation

EU The EU Position

Directive 2010/63/EU separates establishment authorisation from project authorisation, and treats both as the responsibility of a “competent authority” that each Member State must designate, with discretion to delegate specific tasks, including project authorisation itself, to other bodies (art 59). What does not travel with that delegation, however, is the substance of the evaluation: Article 36 provides that no project may be carried out without a favourable project evaluation, and Article 38 fixes what that evaluation must weigh: the project's objectives, the severity of procedures involved, and, centrally, a harm-benefit analysis assessing “whether the harm to the animals in terms of suffering, pain and distress is justified by the expected outcome… taking into account ethical considerations” (art 38(2)(d)). Article 38(3) further requires that the evaluation draw on expertise in the scientific field of use, experimental design, the veterinary care of the relevant species, and animal husbandry. The decision itself is time-bound: Article 41 requires that applicants be notified within 40 working days of a complete application, extendable once by up to 15 working days where the project's complexity or multidisciplinary character justifies it.

Feeding into this decision, but structurally separate from it, is the animal-welfare body every breeder, supplier, and user establishment must set up under Article 26, which must comprise at minimum the person(s) responsible for animal welfare and care and, for users, a scientific member, with input from the establishment's designated veterinarian. Its Article 27 tasks are advisory: guidance to staff on welfare, acquisition, and 3Rs application. It does not authorise projects; it feeds the establishment's own practice and, indirectly, the information available to whichever body performs the Article 38 evaluation.

TR The Turkish Position

The yönetmelik's authorisation architecture is visibly split across at least three actors, each with a distinct and non-overlapping remit, and a fourth once aquatic species are counted. Facility-level licensing runs through the Komisyon (two uzman veteriner hekim from the Ministry's provincial veterinary institute), which inspects premises and issues the kuruluş izni (one-year establishment permit) and, following a further inspection, the çalışma izni (ten-year operating permit) (madde 11–12). Procedures conducted outside a licensed user establishment require separate, direct Ministry permission, obtained by the project's proje yöneticisi submitting a project summary, together with the number and species of animals and the location where the procedure will occur (madde 19). Procedures conducted within a licensed user establishment, by contrast, fall under the case-by-case authority of the yerel etik kurul (HADYEK): it can override the default severity ceiling for procedures involving prolonged, unrelievable severe pain (madde 22(2)–(3)); authorise the reuse of previously used animals under defined conditions (madde 23(2)); grant exceptions permitting the sourcing of wild-caught animals (madde 17(2)); and, under madde 26(5), holds the operative authority over how the alternative-method, reduction, and refinement principle is applied at the procedure level, though not over breeding, husbandry, or housing questions, which the article reserves elsewhere.

Structurally, the closest Turkish parallel to the EU's Article 26 animal-welfare body is the hayvan refahı birimi that every producer, supplier, user, and research-authorised institution must establish (madde 8). Its composition mirrors the EU model closely: at least one veteriner hekim, veteriner sağlık teknikeri, or veteriner sağlık teknisyeni responsible for welfare and care, plus, in user establishments, a member who is also a yerel etik kurul üyesi (madde 8(2)). Its tasks (madde 9) are advisory in the same register as Article 27: staff guidance on welfare, on 3Rs application, and on monitoring project outcomes for their effects on animals.

HADYEK's own legal basis sits a level above the welfare yönetmelik, in primary legislation. Article 9 of Law No. 5199 (Hayvanları Koruma Kanunu, 2004) provides that institutions conducting animal experiments may do so only “through ethics committees established within their own body” (“kendi bünyelerinde kurulmuş ve kurulacak etik kurullar yoluyla”), confirming HADYEK as an institution-level body created by statute, not by the welfare yönetmelik. The same article, in its next paragraph, delegates HADYEK's composition and operating procedure to a further yönetmelik: “Etik kurulların kuruluşu, çalışma usul ve esasları… Bakanlıkça çıkarılacak yönetmelikle belirlenir” (Law No. 5199, art 9). The same paragraph requires the Ministry to consult the Ministry of Health and other relevant bodies before issuing it. That instrument is the Hayvan Deneyleri Etik Kurullarının Çalışma Usul ve Esaslarına Dair Yönetmelik (RG 15.02.2014, No. 28914), issued under Kanun arts 9 and 17. It replaced a 2006 regulation of the same name (RG 06.07.2006, No. 26220) (Uludağ, 2019, p. 1408; Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017). This paper has not read the 2014 text or its later amendments directly, so what follows relies on secondary accounts. Those accounts agree on the structure: a central committee (HADMEK) sets ethical principles and supervises local committees, and a HADYEK may be set up only where the institution has a Ministry-licensed animal unit. No experiment may run without both a HADYEK and an animal-welfare unit, and researchers may not begin without HADYEK approval (Uludağ, 2019, pp. 1408–1409). HADMEK is further tasked with inspecting the experimental conditions and laboratories of the establishments local committees serve, setting the rules for training programmes, judging the equivalence of certificates, and collecting and publishing annual national statistics (Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017). The system is older than the current text: HADMEK held its first meeting on 10 July 2007, and one central and 73 local committees were operating by August 2009 (İzmirli et al., 2010; HADMEK, 2015). HADMEK's own reports trace the growth since: 110 local committees had approved procedural rules in November 2015, though only 90 could work because the rest lacked a Ministry-licensed animal unit; there were 95 in May 2017 and 119 in February 2021. HADMEK meets about four times a year, 61 times in all by February 2021 (HADMEK, 2015, 2017, 2021). Most local committees had to re-approve their rules after the 2014 regulation, whose art 24(2) gave them two years to comply (HADMEK, 2017).

Aquatic research runs through a parallel system. Under the 2019 aquatic-vertebrate regulation, the Directorate-General of Fisheries and Aquaculture issues five-year operating permits, and local aquatic committees (SOCYEK) operate under a separate central committee (SOCMEK) that approves their procedural rules and began work in 2023. A SOCYEK decides applications in one of four ways (suitable, needs correction, conditionally suitable, not suitable) and may approve a project for up to three years (Demirel & Uluköy, 2025, pp. 116–119). The same authors describe recurring confusion in practice over which activities count as a “procedure” needing approval, such as egg and sperm stripping from broodstock (p. 122).

The welfare yönetmelik's own authority also traces to art 9, in its final paragraph: the breeding, feeding, housing and care of experimental animals; the registration (tescil) of breeder, supplier and user establishments; the qualifications of staff; the records to be kept; which species may be bred; and the rules those establishments must follow are all to be set by a Ministry yönetmelik (Law No. 5199, art 9(6)). “Bakanlık” throughout is the Tarım ve Orman Bakanlığı (art 3(o)). The Komisyon's licensing role in madde 11–12 is therefore the implementation of a statutory registration mandate, not a free-standing regulatory choice. One Kanun-level control sits outside this structure altogether: the import and export of experimental animals requires a Ministry permit (art 10(7)), a requirement with no counterpart in Directive 2010/63/EU, which leaves cross-border movement to EU animal-health law.

What this leaves open is precisely what Article 9 delegates but does not itself supply: HADYEK's membership and quorum. The load-bearing question for a hiring manager assessing a facility's authorisation posture is whether HADYEK's project evaluation carries anything equivalent to Article 38's harm-benefit analysis or Article 41's statutory decision clock. Law No. 5199 art 9 does establish substantive tests directly, at statutory level. Animals may not be used in non-scientific diagnosis, treatment or experiments (art 9(1)). Experiments must be carried out in a way that protects the animals, and the animals must be properly cared for and housed (art 9(2)). Experimental use is permitted only “in the absence of any other option” (“başkaca bir seçenek olmaması halinde”) (art 9(3)), a replacement-principle statement embedded in primary legislation rather than in implementing regulation, which is a structurally different legislative placement from the EU's Article 4 (addressed in Section 4). These are threshold conditions, not an evaluation method: art 9 says nothing about severity, reduction, a harm-benefit weighing, the expertise reviewers must bring, or a timeline, and neither does the welfare yönetmelik. Whether Türkiye's project-evaluation stage matches Article 38's substance or is closer to a procedural check remains unverified. None of the secondary accounts read for this paper reports a harm-benefit test or a decision deadline in the 2014 regulation, but only a direct reading of that text can settle the point.

The EU benchmark is itself less uniform than Article 38's wording suggests. A cross-national study found that France, Germany, Finland, Denmark and Sweden reworded the harm-benefit requirement on transposition. Denmark, for instance, speaks of a project's “usefulness” and omits ethical considerations, and Finland drops the outcome element. National guidance on carrying out the analysis existed only in France, the Netherlands, Switzerland and the UK, plus one regional document for Brussels (Hajosi & Grimm, 2024, pp. 6–9, 19–20). The contrast with Türkiye is therefore between a requirement that binds but is operationalised unevenly and one that, on current evidence, is not stated at all.

04

Severity Classification and the 3Rs: Binding or Aspirational?

Textually, the two regimes are close to identical. Directive art 4(1)–(3) states replacement, reduction, and refinement as binding obligations on Member States; the yönetmelik's madde 26 mirrors the same three-part structure, and Kanun art 9 independently states two of the three at the level of primary legislation: replacement, “in the absence of any other option” (art 9(3)), and a general refinement-type duty to conduct experiments protectively and to care for and house the animals appropriately (art 9(2)). Reduction has no statutory anchor and exists only in the yönetmelik. This is a placement the Directive's own 3Rs principle does not have (art 4 sits in secondary EU law, transposed into national implementing rules). Severity classification shows the same convergence: Directive art 15 requires every procedure classified case-by-case as non-recovery, mild, moderate, or severe using Annex VIII criteria; madde 22 requires the identical four-tier classification (düzelmez/hafif/orta/şiddetli) using Ek-8 criteria. Method-choice and anaesthesia obligations (madde 20–21) track Directive arts 13–14 comparably closely.

One genuine divergence survives this convergence, and it is institutional rather than definitional. Directive art 15(2) prohibits procedures involving severe, long-lasting, unrelievable pain outright; an exception requires a Member State to invoke the art 55(3) safeguard clause: a “provisional measure” the state must adopt and immediately report to the European Commission and every other Member State. Madde 22(3) permits the same exception, but grants the power to any individual yerel etik kurul, case-by-case, on documented scientific grounds, with no notification mechanism located in this research. The EU treats this decision as significant enough to require international visibility; Türkiye treats it as a local committee's discretionary call.

Beyond that specific divergence, the honest conclusion is that statutory text does not distinguish these regimes at this layer: both bind on paper. What “binding” means is itself contested. Grimm and Dusseldorp argue that the 3Rs are principles in the norm-theoretical sense, commands to realise their content to the highest degree possible, measured against the Directive's stated goal of full replacement (recital 10). Read that way, they call for more than incremental improvement on an initial design (Grimm & Dusseldorp, 2025, pp. 557–560). Neither regime's text says which reading its review bodies should apply. Whether the classification and 3Rs obligations are applied with comparable rigor is an enforcement-capacity question, not a drafting one, and it is addressed with the relevant evidence in Section 6.

05

Personnel Competence: Article 23 and Its Turkish Mirror

The convergence pattern from Sections 2 and 4 repeats here. Directive art 23 requires adequate education and training before performing any of four functions: carrying out procedures, designing procedures and projects, caring for animals, and killing animals; designers must additionally hold discipline-relevant training and species-specific knowledge, and staff in the other three functions work under supervision until competence is demonstrated (art 23(1)–(2)). Madde 32 reproduces this exact four-function structure (prosedür uygulamak, prosedür/proje tasarlamak, hayvanlara bakmak, hayvanları öldürmek) with the same designer-specific and supervision-until-competent rules (madde 32(2)–(3)). Article 23(3) requires Member States to publish minimum training requirements against Annex V; madde 32(4) points to an Ek-10 (Annex 10) as its training-content basis, suggesting a structurally parallel mechanism, though this paper has not verified Ek-10's substantive content against Annex V.

One divergence is textually clear rather than merely unverified: Article 23 ties competence entirely to demonstrated, published-standard training, with no tenure-based substitute. Madde 32(4) creates a dual pathway for the sorumlu yönetici (facility manager) role specifically: two or more years' pre-2013 experience in deney hayvanları or Ek-10 training completion satisfies the requirement, meaning tenure predating the current training framework can stand in for it. Whether Ek-10's content is FELASA-equivalent in rigor is one of the open questions in Section 8; that a tenure-based exemption exists at all is established by the text itself. The aquatic regulation widens the gap: a responsible manager qualifies with two years' experience or a user certificate, and a university manager with a doctorate needs no further training (Demirel & Uluköy, 2025, p. 118).

Certification is also decentralised. Researchers must hold a laboratory-animal user certificate, earned through an 80-hour course (Uludağ, 2019, p. 1409; İzmirli et al., 2010). The certificate is issued by the local HADYEK that runs the course, with HADMEK judging equivalence between certificates (Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017). Competence in Türkiye is thus certified institution by institution, which helps explain why no systemic mapping onto FELASA categories exists (Section 1). The course content is set not by the regulation but by a 2007 ministerial circular (Genelge 2007/11). It defines three categories: A1 for animal caretakers (31 hours), and A2 for technicians and B for researchers (40 hours of theory plus 40 of practice each) (HADMEK, 2015). HADMEK acknowledged in 2017 that the circular had not been updated to the Directive and began revising it, with a new circular expected from 2018 (HADMEK, 2017). Whether that revision was issued is not established here.

The Kanun adds two points the yönetmelik alone does not show. First, madde 32's competence regime has a statutory anchor: art 9(6) expressly delegates “çalışan personelin nitelikleri” (the qualifications of staff) to Ministry regulation, and personnel status has penalty consequences, since art 28 doubles any administrative fine where the offender is a veterinarian, veterinary health technician, or a person tasked with caring for or protecting animals, a category that on its face covers animal-care staff in a user establishment. Second, art 7(1) provides, without qualification, that medical and surgical interventions on animals are carried out only by veterinarians (“sadece veteriner hekimler tarafından yapılır”), and art 28(c) attaches a per-animal fine to breaches. Directive art 23 has no equivalent: surgical procedures may be performed by any person whose competence has been demonstrated. The contrast runs deeper: the Directive does not even reserve the designated-veterinarian role for veterinarians, allowing a suitably qualified “expert” instead where more appropriate (art 25), an option some veterinary commentators want removed (Kiraga & Dzikowski, 2023, p. 8). Whether art 7 reaches surgical procedures performed by non-veterinarian researchers under an ethics-committee-approved project, or whether art 9 and the welfare yönetmelik operate as the specific rule for experiments, is not answered by the Kanun's text and is carried to Section 8.

06

Inspection, Enforcement, and Reporting Obligations

This is where Section 4's enforcement-gap claim gets tested against text, and it holds up. Internationally, inspection systems vary widely and are not always seen as effectively implemented, and sanctions range from fines through imprisonment to withdrawal of authority or accreditation (Bayne et al., 2010, p. 111); the question is where Türkiye sits on that range. Directive art 34 fixes a numerical inspection floor: at least one-third of user establishments inspected annually, non-human-primate facilities at least once a year, with an “appropriate proportion” unannounced. Law No. 5199 art 17(1) vests inspection authority in the Bakanlık, delegable to the highest local civil administrator (“mahallin en büyük mülkî amiri”), and art 25 gives that same authority the power to impose administrative fines. Art 17(2) then delegates inspector qualifications, inspection procedure (“denetime ilişkin usul ve esaslar”), a registration-and-monitoring system, and notification duties to a further yönetmelik not located in this research. Frequency is not mentioned at all, not even as a matter the implementing rule must address. Below statute, secondary sources describe several inspecting bodies: HADMEK for experimental conditions and laboratories, each HADYEK for conditions within its own institution, and, for aquatic facilities, the Ministry and SOCMEK (Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017; Demirel & Uluköy, 2025, p. 120). None of them reports a frequency floor. HADMEK's own record is the only published count of inspections found. It lists 33 inspections in 2008–2015: 1 in 2008, 22 in 2009, 2 each in 2010 and 2011, none in 2012, 1 in 2013, none in 2014 and 5 in 2015 (HADMEK, 2015). These are central-committee inspections only. Inspections by the Ministry's licensing Komisyon or by local committees are not counted there. Even so, against roughly 90 active local committees they point to occasional scrutiny, not a cycle, and nothing comparable to the Directive's floor of one-third of user establishments a year. Whatever that instrument specifies, its absence from the primary statute means Türkiye's inspection floor, if one exists, is not verifiable from the Kanun itself.

The penalty structure sharpens the picture. Directive art 60 requires penalties to be “effective, proportionate and dissuasive,” a qualitative standard Member States implement and notify to the Commission, deliberately left open to scale with the violation and the violator. Law No. 5199 art 28(e) instead sets an itemized, per-animal administrative fine for failing to comply with art 9 “and the yönetmelikler to be issued under it,” which brings breaches of the welfare yönetmelik itself within the fine: 1,200 TRY per animal for general non-compliance, 4,500 TRY per animal for conducting experiments without authorization (2021-amended base figures; Turkish administrative fines are typically revalued annually by statute, so the current amount is almost certainly higher, though this paper has not verified the current figure). Two features of this schedule matter more than the exact number. First, it scales along the wrong axis for deterrence. The Kanun does include escalators: the fine is multiplied by the number of animals; it is doubled where the offender is a veterinarian, veterinary health technician, or a person tasked with animal care (art 28, penultimate para.); and it rises one-fold on a repeated offence and three-fold on further repetition (art 30). Art 24 adds a non-monetary sanction, a ban on keeping animals and confiscation, for anyone who breaches the Kanun's protective provisions. None of these tracks the violator's resources, so the same schedule applies to a small academic lab and a facility running multinational-sponsored preclinical work. Second, it is exclusively administrative. Law No. 5199 art 28/A, which creates criminal, custodial penalties, covers killing an endangered animal or destroying a species' lineage, intentionally killing or torturing an ev hayvanı or evcil hayvan, sexual assault on animals, and animal fighting. None of its offences is framed around experimental use, and prosecution under it requires a written application from the Ministry's provincial or district directorate. Experimental-animal violations therefore remain, in ordinary reading, a kabahat (administrative infraction), not a suç (crime). One interpretive edge is left open: art 28/A(2) carries no scientific-use carve-out, and whether a purpose-bred laboratory animal could ever count as an “evcil hayvan” (defined in art 3(e) as an animal “cultured and trained” by humans) is unaddressed. The “trained” element makes that unlikely for most laboratory species.

One further enforcement gap is visible only in the Kanun. The import and export permit for experimental animals (art 10(7)) has no matching fine: art 28(f) sanctions the first, fourth, fifth, sixth and eighth paragraphs of art 10, but not the seventh.

On reporting, the section heading's third term, the Directive requires public-facing transparency, and Regulation (EU) 2019/1010 strengthened it. Since 2021, non-technical project summaries go to the Commission within six months of authorisation for an open-access, searchable EU database. Statistics are now submitted electronically and published annually in a database, and the former Article 57 report was deleted (Busquet, 2020, pp. 168–169). Even the EU figures are incomplete: about 8 million animals falling under the Directive, alongside roughly 10 million counted each year, were not included in the headline count (Busquet, 2020, p. 167).

Türkiye has the aggregate half of this. The Kanun provides only the infrastructure for records, through art 9(6) (records establishments must keep) and art 17(2) (a registration-and-monitoring system and notification duties), and imposes no duty to publish. Below statute, local committees send annual statistics to HADMEK, which is tasked with publishing national figures (Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017). HADMEK has collected data from local committees since 2010 and published it in multi-year reports: 2010–2014 (issued 2015), 2015–2016 (2017), 2017 (2018) and 2018–2020 (2021). An earlier, unpublished central-committee report counted 148,957 animals in 2008 (İzmirli et al., 2010). The 2010–2014 report promised annual reports from 2015, but none covering a year after 2020 has been found (HADMEK, 2015).

Second, HADMEK itself warned that the data are not comparable with the EU's. What Türkiye lacks is project-level transparency: there is no counterpart to non-technical summaries. For aquatic research the aquatic regulation's art 30 goes further, barring establishments from disclosing their studies and procedures to the public and leaving any disclosure to the Ministry (Demirel & Uluköy, 2025, p. 120). This closes the loop opened in Section 1. A per-animal administrative fine that never scales with the violator's resources is a materially weaker deterrent for an internationally-linked facility than the loss of AAALAC accreditation or a sponsor's contract. This is precisely why compliance in that context tracks accreditation and sponsor pressure rather than the statutory penalty regime.

07

Where Turkish Law Diverges, and the Practical Consequence

A reader who compares only the substantive standards (which animals and procedures are covered, what the 3Rs principle requires, how severity is classified, what functions demand trained personnel) will find the yönetmelik reads as a faithful transposition of Directive 2010/63/EU, article for madde, in most places. That similarity is real, but it is also where the risk lies for anyone doing due diligence on a Turkish facility: the statutory text invites a confidence the institutional layer doesn't support.

The divergences this paper could verify cluster at three points, all downstream of the text itself. First, decision authority is fragmented and only partly documented. Project-level ethical review runs through HADYEK, or for aquatic species through a separate SOCYEK system. Their statutory basis (Law No. 5199, art 9) and governing regulations are now identified, but no published source reports their evaluation criteria or decision timeline. Second, the one clear textual divergence is institutional: the severe-pain exception is an institution-level HADYEK call in Türkiye, versus a Member-State safeguard clause requiring Commission notification in the EU. This moves a high-stakes decision from an internationally visible register to a local, undocumented one. Third, enforcement is verifiably weaker on the specific dimension that matters for deterrence: no located inspection-frequency floor (the Kanun does not even name frequency as a delegated matter), a per-animal administrative fine whose escalators track repetition and professional status but never a facility's size or sponsor relationships, and aggregate statistics that are published late while project-level information is not published at all.

The practical consequence for a hiring manager or compliance officer: reading the yönetmelik tells you what a facility is supposed to do, not what it actually does. The Directive's authority chain is auditable end to end: competent authority, evaluation criteria, inspection floor, penalty standard, public statistics, all in the same instrument or its designated delegate. Türkiye's chain has the same shape but breaks at exactly the points an auditor would need to check. That gap is filled, in practice, by the channels Section 1 identified (AAALAC accreditation status, sponsor audit history, and a facility's own track record) rather than by the statute. Due diligence on a Turkish facility therefore cannot stop at the regulatory text; it has to reach the facility-specific evidence the text itself doesn't produce.

08

Open Questions and Direction of Travel

The earlier draft of this paper listed three missing documents. One is now identified: the ethics-committee yönetmelik (RG 15.02.2014, No. 28914), issued under Kanun arts 9 and 17, which may also be the instrument through which art 17's inspection delegation is exercised for experimental animals. It still needs to be read in its current, amended form, because the secondary accounts used here predate any later amendments. The remaining gaps are Ek-10's actual training content, needed to assess whether it is substantively equivalent to Directive Annex V or FELASA-recognized frameworks, and HADMEK statistics for years after 2020, which the reports read for this revision do not cover. Reviewing these would resolve the paper's remaining pending rows and either confirm or complicate the enforcement-gap argument in Sections 4 and 6. The Kanun indicates they should exist: Transitional Art 2 required every yönetmelik the Law calls for to be prepared within one year of its entry into force in July 2004, and the 2021 amendments (Law No. 7332) set a six-month deadline for regulations under the amended text (Law No. 5199, geçici madde 2; geçici madde 3, last para.). The literature search for this revision confirmed this: the delegated instruments exist, and the gap was in sourcing, not in the legislation.

A different gap is in the research itself. No study found for this paper examines how HADYEKs work in practice: approval rates, turnaround times, or what reviewers actually weigh. Descriptive and legal accounts exist (e.g., Gölcü & Aksoy, 2017; Uludağ, 2019; Demirel & Uluköy, 2025), but the empirical layer this paper's argument turns on is unstudied. That is itself a finding, and the natural next study.

The full-text reading of Law No. 5199 adds two interpretive questions that only practice or case law can settle. First, whether the veterinarian-only rule for medical and surgical interventions (art 7(1)) constrains who may perform surgical procedures in an approved project, which would be a substantial divergence from Directive art 23 in the opposite direction, stricter rather than looser. Second, whether any experimental-animal conduct could fall within the criminal offences of art 28/A, which contain no scientific-use exception. A due-diligence review of a Turkish facility should ask how its HADYEK and its legal counsel read both.

The direction-of-travel question is harder to answer than it first appears. Accession negotiations have been stalled since 2018, yet the welfare yönetmelik, dated to the more active 2011–2014 negotiating period, remains a close textual match to the Directive at nearly every layer this paper could check, including recent EU-side detail like the cephalopod-inclusion and needle-prick thresholds. Whether that reflects ongoing technical alignment independent of accession politics, or a high-water mark frozen at the moment negotiations stalled and not revisited since, is itself unverified: the Directive has been amended since 2014 (Annex IV's killing-methods update in 2024, for instance), and whether Türkiye's implementing text tracks such amendments is a live question this research did not resolve. The international-law channel is weaker still: Türkiye signed the Council of Europe's Convention ETS No. 123 on vertebrate animals used for experimental purposes in 1986, but it has not entered into force for Türkiye (Akben, 2023). Alignment has run through domestic regulation modelled on EU law, not through treaty obligation. For a facility weighing long-term compliance investment, that uncertainty, not the current text, may be the more consequential unknown.
